# Assessment: TRANSEURO Trial Design and Patient Selection Adequacy

## Overall judgment
The evidence base supports a **cautious, conditional justification** for TRANSEURO to proceed, but three substantial concerns temper confidence that the trial design and patient selection are fully adequate given prior findings.

---

## Top Three Concerns

### 1. Loss of blinded/placebo control undermines ability to detect a true treatment effect
**Concern:** The two most methodologically rigorous prior trials were double-blind, sham-surgery-controlled (Freed et al., PMID 11236774; Olanow et al., PMID 12953276), and both failed to show a significant *overall* treatment effect on primary endpoints — benefit was confined to subgroups (younger patients in Freed; milder-disease patients in Olanow). Critically, these blinded results contradicted the more favorable outcomes seen in earlier open-label series (PMID 2786405, 1435880, 7700284), a discrepancy explicitly flagged by reviewers as reflecting placebo/expectation effects intrinsic to surgical trials (PMID 20887880, 20887876). TRANSEURO is described as an **open-label** trial (PMID 25170676, 20489615), which reintroduces exactly the ascertainment bias that the field's most informative prior data (the RCTs) were designed to eliminate. Without a concurrent sham arm, distinguishing genuine graft-derived motor benefit from placebo response, rater expectation, or natural fluctuation will be difficult, especially given known regression-to-mean and Hawthorne effects in PD surgical trials.

**Confidence: High.** This is a well-documented, structural design limitation directly attributable via citation, not inference.

---

### 2. Graft-induced dyskinesia (GID) risk is better characterized mechanistically but not demonstrably solved
**Concern:** Off-medication dyskinesia affected up to 56% of transplanted patients in the Olanow trial (PMID 12953276) and was also seen in Freed's cohort (PMID 11236774), representing the single most serious safety signal from prior human trials. Substantial preclinical work since then has identified plausible contributing factors — uneven/patchy striatal reinnervation (PMID 16256359), graft size (PMID 16406222), pre-graft L-DOPA priming (PMID 19399877, 19393238), and serotonergic-to-dopaminergic cell ratio in the graft (PMID 22579773) — and these have informed proposed mitigation strategies (refined graft placement, tissue composition, patient selection) (PMID 20868333, 20887881, 21907087, 23195418, 23828589, 21901584). However, **none of these mitigation strategies has been prospectively validated in a controlled human trial** prior to TRANSEURO; they are extrapolated from rodent GID models whose translational fidelity to human GID remains uncertain (PMID 20868333 explicitly notes the "lack of a truly representative animal model"). TRANSEURO is therefore proceeding on a mechanistically informed but clinically unproven risk-mitigation package.

**Confidence: Moderate-to-high.** The mechanistic rationale is reasonably strong, but the absence of prior in-human validation of the specific countermeasures leaves real residual uncertainty about whether GID incidence will actually be reduced.

---

### 3. Patient selection criteria show both a rationally-motivated shift toward likely responders and a documented, unaddressed selection/equity bias
**Concern:** Age- and severity-stratified analyses from Freed (PMID 11236774) and Olanow (PMID 12953276) support enriching for younger, milder-disease patients — a rationale TRANSEURO appears to have adopted. However, direct empirical data from TRANSEURO's own Cambridge cohort (PMID 25170676) show that enrolled patients differed systematically from eligible-but-unenrolled PD patients: they were younger, more educated, had higher cognitive scores (memory, fluency, visuospatial), better baseline motor function but *more* motor complications, and were disproportionately drawn from patients already under the principal investigator's care. This raises two distinct issues: (a) a legitimate scientific question of whether the enriched sample will actually be representative of the broader population the therapy would eventually need to serve, limiting generalizability of efficacy findings; and (b) a practical/ethical access concern (referral bias) that is neither a strength nor an addressed limitation of the current protocol, but a documented empirical fact about how selection is actually occurring at at least one site.

**Confidence: Moderate.** The direction of selection (toward likely responders) is scientifically defensible given subgroup data, but the magnitude and consequences of the documented selection bias are not resolved, and the source (single-center, n=26 vs n=33) limits how strongly this generalizes across all TRANSEURO sites.

---

## Secondary caveat (lower priority but noted)
Evidence of host-to-graft α-synuclein/Lewy pathology propagation in long-term graft survivors (PMID 18391963, 21245577, 20846907, 21626551, 22936307) raises a legitimate long-term durability question, but this is a decade-plus-horizon concern rather than one that should block near-term initiation of grafting, and it does not by itself indicate inadequate near-term trial design.

## Bottom line
From a purely clinical-evidence perspective, TRANSEURO's rationale is grounded in real, if incompletely validated, mechanistic advances since the 2001/2003 sham-controlled failures. However, the **combination of an unblinded design (concern 1) with unvalidated-in-humans GID mitigation strategies (concern 2) and empirically documented selection skew (concern 3)** means the trial is reasonably justified as a hypothesis-refining study, but the design leaves meaningful risk that its efficacy conclusions will be difficult to interpret rigorously and that safety improvements may not fully materialize as anticipated.
